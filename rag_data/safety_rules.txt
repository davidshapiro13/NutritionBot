# Food Safety Rules: Illness, Pathogens & Prevention
# Sources: CDC, FDA, USDA FSIS, WHO
# Last updated: 2026-04-02

================================================================================
SECTION 1: FOOD POISONING SYMPTOMS & WHEN TO SEEK HELP
================================================================================

Source: CDC — https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/signs-symptoms/index.html

The five primary symptoms of food poisoning (foodborne illness):
- Diarrhea
- Stomach pain or cramps
- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Fever

IMPORTANT: The food that caused illness is often NOT the last food eaten.
It frequently takes 2–3 days to feel sick after eating contaminated food.

Symptom Onset Timeline by Pathogen:
- Staphylococcus aureus (Staph): 30 minutes to 8 hours
- Salmonella: 6 hours to 6 days
- Norovirus: 12 to 48 hours
- E. coli (STEC): 3 to 4 days (range: 2–8 days)
- Listeria: up to 2 weeks
- Clostridium perfringens: 6 to 24 hours
- Campylobacter: 2 to 5 days

SEEK MEDICAL CARE IMMEDIATELY IF YOU HAVE:
1. Bloody diarrhea
2. Diarrhea lasting more than 3 days
3. High fever — temperature above 102°F (38.9°C)
4. Uncontrollable vomiting (cannot keep any liquids down)
5. Signs of dehydration:
   - Very little or no urination
   - Very dry mouth and throat
   - Dizziness when standing
   - Dark-colored urine
   - Crying without tears (in children)

SPECIAL AT-RISK POPULATIONS — Contact a doctor promptly if:
- Pregnant women develop fever and flu-like symptoms (risk of Listeria)
- Children under 5 show bloody stool, high fever, or dehydration
- Adults 65 or older experience prolonged or worsening symptoms
- Immunocompromised individuals experience any food poisoning symptoms

HUS EMERGENCY WARNING SIGNS (especially after E. coli infection):
Go to the emergency room immediately:
- Decreased or stopped urination
- Extreme paleness
- Unexplained bruising or bleeding
- Blood in urine
- Extreme fatigue or confusion

WHAT TO DO WHEN SICK:
1. Stay hydrated — sip water, clear broth, or oral rehydration solutions (Pedialyte)
2. Avoid anti-diarrheal medications (Imodium) without doctor advice, especially
   with bloody diarrhea or high fever — can worsen E. coli infection
3. Consult a healthcare provider — stool/blood test can identify pathogen
4. Document meals — record everything eaten in the past week
5. Report illness — contact local/state health department if you suspect a source

SECTION 2: COMMON FOODBORNE PATHOGENS
================================================================================

--- SALMONELLA ---
Source: FDA / CDC

Leading cause of foodborne illness hospitalizations and deaths in the US (~450 deaths/year).
Onset: 12–72 hours. Duration: 4–7 days.
Common sources: raw or undercooked meat, poultry, eggs; unpasteurized dairy;
                raw sprouts; nut butters.
At-risk: children under 5, adults 65+, pregnant women, immunocompromised.
Prevention: cook poultry to 165°F, eggs until yolk and white are firm;
            wash surfaces; avoid raw sprouts if high-risk.

--- NOROVIRUS ---
Source: CDC

Most common cause of foodborne illness in the US (21 million/year).
Onset: 12–48 hours. Duration: 1–3 days.
Contagious: infected people can spread virus for 2+ weeks after recovery.
Common sources: raw shellfish (especially oysters), contaminated produce,
                direct contact with infected person.
IMPORTANT: Hand sanitizer is less effective — use soap and water.
Prevention: wash hands with soap and water; cook shellfish thoroughly;
            stay home 48 hours after symptoms stop.

--- E. COLI (STEC / O157:H7) ---
Source: CDC

Onset: 2–8 days (typically 3–4 days). Duration: 5–7 days.
Symptoms: severe stomach cramps, bloody or watery diarrhea, vomiting.
Serious complication: Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS) — life-threatening,
especially in young children. See HUS warning signs above.
DO NOT take Imodium with bloody diarrhea or high fever.
Common sources: ground beef, leafy greens, unpasteurized milk/juice.
Prevention: cook ground beef to 160°F; wash produce; avoid raw milk.

--- LISTERIA ---
Especially dangerous for pregnant women and newborns.
Can survive and grow at refrigerator temperatures (40°F).
Found in: deli meats, hot dogs, soft cheeses (brie, feta, queso fresco),
          smoked seafood, raw sprouts.
Can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, or serious illness in newborns.
Pregnant women should avoid deli meats unless heated steaming hot (165°F).

--- CAMPYLOBACTER ---
One of the most common causes of diarrheal illness worldwide.
Onset: 2–5 days. Duration: 2–10 days.
Mainly linked to raw or undercooked poultry.
Even a tiny drop of raw chicken juice can cause infection.

--- CLOSTRIDIUM PERFRINGENS ---
Often from large batches of food kept warm too long (buffets, cafeteria-style).
Onset: 6–24 hours. Usually self-limiting.
Prevention: keep hot food above 140°F; don't hold cooked food in the danger zone.

--- STAPHYLOCOCCUS AUREUS (STAPH) ---
Fastest onset: 30 minutes to 8 hours.
Caused by heat-stable toxins in improperly stored food — cooking does NOT
destroy the toxin once it forms.
Linked to: salads, sandwiches, dairy products, foods handled with bare hands.

SECTION 3: THE FOUR CORE PREVENTION STEPS (USDA Framework)
================================================================================

Source: USDA FSIS

STEP 1: CLEAN — Wash Hands and Surfaces Often
- Wash hands with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds:
  before and after handling food, after using bathroom, after touching pets
- Wash cutting boards, utensils, countertops with hot soapy water after
  contact with raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs
- Rinse fruits and vegetables under running water
- Sanitize with bleach solution: 1 tbsp unscented bleach per gallon of water

STEP 2: SEPARATE — Don't Cross-Contaminate
- Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood, and eggs away from ready-to-eat foods
- Use separate cutting boards for raw meat vs. produce
- Store raw meat on the bottom refrigerator shelf in sealed containers
- Never put cooked food on a plate that held raw meat without washing it first
- Bag raw meats separately at the grocery store

STEP 3: COOK — Cook to the Right Temperature
Safe Minimum Internal Temperatures (USDA):
- Ground beef, pork, lamb, veal:              160°F (71°C)
- Beef/pork/lamb/veal steaks, roasts, chops:  145°F (63°C) + 3-min rest
- All poultry (chicken, turkey, duck):        165°F (74°C)
- Fish and shellfish:                         145°F (63°C)
- Eggs:                                       cook until yolk and white are firm
- Egg dishes and casseroles:                  160°F (71°C)
- Leftovers:                                  reheat to 165°F (74°C)
Always use a food thermometer — color is NOT a reliable safety indicator.

STEP 4: CHILL — Refrigerate Promptly
- Refrigerate perishables within 2 hours (1 hour if temperature above 90°F)
- Never thaw food at room temperature
- Keep refrigerator at or below 40°F; freezer at or below 0°F

================================================================================
SECTION 4: HIGH-RISK FOODS
================================================================================

Handle these with extra care:
- Raw or undercooked poultry, meat, seafood, or eggs
- Raw sprouts (alfalfa, bean, clover, radish)
- Unwashed raw produce
- Cut melon left at room temperature
- Unpasteurized dairy products and juices
- Raw dough or batter (uncooked flour or raw eggs are both risky)
- Deli meats and hot dogs (Listeria risk — heat to 165°F if pregnant or high-risk)
- Soft cheeses made with unpasteurized milk

SECTION 5: US & GLOBAL STATISTICS
================================================================================

US (CDC):
- 48 million people sickened annually
- 128,000 hospitalizations annually
- 3,000 deaths annually

Most common causes (US):
1. Norovirus — most illnesses (21 million/year)
2. Salmonella — most hospitalizations and deaths
3. Clostridium perfringens
4. Campylobacter
5. Staphylococcus aureus

Global (WHO):
- 600 million people (1 in 10) sickened each year
- 420,000 deaths each year
- Children under 5: 40% of global foodborne disease burden
